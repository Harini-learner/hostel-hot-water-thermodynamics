# Complete 28-Heading Thermodynamic Study

> **Project basis:** Seven girls’ hostels, 58 distributed electric storage water heaters, two observed brands (V-Guard and Venus). Field values are clearly distinguished from reference/calculated values.

## 1. Introduction to the Hostel Hot-Water System
The girls' hostel complex contains seven hostels with a total of 58 individual electric storage water heaters. The arrangement is distributed: each restroom/local facility has its own heater rather than one central boiler. The observed distribution is H1=8, H2=8, H3=8, H4=8, H5=16, H6=4 and H7=6. Two brands were observed: V-Guard and Venus. A representative heater specification is 25 L capacity and 2 kW rated power. The study applies thermodynamic principles to real observations of approximately 37 °C normal water and more than 45 °C heated outlet water.

## 2. Thermodynamic System, Surroundings and Boundary
For detailed analysis, one storage heater and its water are taken as the representative control system. The water, tank, heating element and associated thermal processes form the system of interest. The bathroom air, walls, external pipe surfaces and electrical environment are surroundings. Electrical energy crosses the boundary into the heater, while useful hot water and heat losses cross the boundary during operation. At hostel level, the total system is the collection of the 58 local heater units.

## 3. Open, Closed and Isolated System
A heater during water withdrawal is approximately an open system because mass and energy cross its boundary. During a storage period with no water withdrawal, the stored water can be treated approximately as a closed-system portion for transient heating analysis. The actual hostel heater is not isolated because electrical energy enters and heat is transferred to the surroundings. This distinction is important when selecting the First Law or SFEE form.

## 4. Working Substance – Water
Water is the working substance because it is heated and supplied for domestic use. The field measurement found normal/inlet water at approximately 37 °C and heated outlet water above 45 °C. Water has a high specific heat capacity, so it can store substantial thermal energy. For a 25 L storage volume, the water mass is approximately 25 kg when the tank is full, using a density of about 1 kg/L.

## 5. Thermodynamic State and Properties
The state of the water is described by temperature, pressure, volume, density, internal energy and enthalpy. A representative storage capacity is 25 L. The field temperature condition changes from approximately 37 °C before heating to more than 45 °C at the outlet. The exact state inside the tank cannot be inferred solely from the outlet measurement, so the report distinguishes measured outlet temperature from assumed tank-average temperature.

## 6. Intensive and Extensive Properties
Temperature, pressure, density and specific heat are intensive properties because they do not depend on the amount of water. Mass, volume and total internal energy are extensive properties because they depend on the quantity of water. For the representative 25 L unit, volume is approximately 25 L and mass is approximately 25 kg when full.

## 7. Thermodynamic Equilibrium
Thermal equilibrium is reached when water and the surrounding parts of the storage volume have no significant temperature difference driving internal heat transfer. During frequent withdrawal, complete equilibrium may not exist because hot water leaves and fresh water enters. The observation of approximately 1 minute availability during frequent use and approximately 5 minutes after prolonged non-use shows that operating history affects the transient thermal condition.

## 8. Zeroth Law of Thermodynamics
The Zeroth Law establishes the basis of temperature measurement. A thermometer placed in thermal contact with water can indicate the water temperature after reaching thermal equilibrium. The field study used temperature measurement to observe approximately 37 °C normal water and more than 45 °C heated water. These measurements provide the thermal-state information used in the heat calculation.

## 9. Temperature Measurement and Temperature Scales
The Celsius scale is used for the field observations. Conversion to Kelvin is T(K)=T(°C)+273.15. Thus 37 °C corresponds to 310.15 K and the 45 °C reference corresponds to 318.15 K. The minimum observed temperature rise is ΔT=45−37=8 K because the actual heated temperature was greater than 45 °C.

## 10. Thermodynamic Processes
The heater undergoes electrical heating, storage, water withdrawal, replacement of withdrawn water and cooling/standby heat loss. During heating, electrical energy is converted into internal energy of the water. During withdrawal, hot water carries energy away and incoming water changes the tank thermal state. During standby, heat is lost to the surroundings even without useful water withdrawal.

## 11. Heat and Work Interactions
Electrical input is treated as work/energy supplied to the heater. The heating element transfers energy to the water as heat. Additional heat transfer occurs from the hot tank and pipes to the surroundings. In simplified form, electrical input is divided into useful energy stored in or delivered by the water and non-useful heat losses.

## 12. First Law of Thermodynamics
The First Law states that energy is conserved. For a simplified heating process, electrical energy supplied to the heater is equal to the useful increase in water energy plus losses. For a closed-system representation, Q−W=ΔU. For flowing operation, an energy balance based on enthalpy and flow is more appropriate.

## 13. Application of First Law to the Hot-Water System
Using a 25 L reference storage volume, m≈25 kg. Taking the measured normal water temperature as 37 °C and using 45 °C as the minimum reference heated temperature gives ΔT=8 K. With cp=4.18 kJ/kg·K, Q=mc_pΔT=25×4.18×8=836 kJ=0.232 kWh. This is a reference useful sensible-heat requirement, not actual electrical consumption, because the exact final tank temperature and meter energy input were not measured.

## 14. Internal Energy and Enthalpy
When water is heated, its internal energy increases. For liquid water over this range, the sensible internal-energy change can be approximated by mc_pΔT. Enthalpy is especially useful when analysing water entering and leaving the heater because flowing streams carry enthalpy. For liquid water, Δh≈c_pΔT is a useful approximation for this study.

## 15. Specific Heat Capacity of Water
The specific heat capacity used is cp≈4.18 kJ/kg·K. It represents the energy required to raise 1 kg of water by 1 K. The high value explains why substantial electrical energy is needed to heat hostel water. For 25 kg and an 8 K reference rise, the heat requirement is 836 kJ.

## 16. Heat Required for Water Heating
The governing equation is Q=mc_p(T2−T1). With m=25 kg, cp=4.18 kJ/kg·K, T1=37 °C and a conservative reference T2=45 °C, Q=836 kJ≈0.232 kWh. Since the measured outlet water was above 45 °C, this result is a minimum/reference value for the stated 25 kg assumption.

## 17. Electrical Energy Input to the Heater
The representative heater rating is 2 kW at 230 V, 50 Hz. Electrical energy is E=Pt. At continuous 2 kW operation, 1 minute corresponds to 0.0333 kWh and 5 minutes corresponds to 0.1667 kWh. However, the observed 1-minute and 5-minute hot-water availability times are not automatically heater ON-times, so these are theoretical full-power reference values only.

## 18. Energy Conversion in the Water Heater
The main energy pathway is electrical energy → heating element → thermal energy → water. Some input energy is stored in the water, while some is transferred to the tank, pipes and surrounding air. The practical heater therefore demonstrates energy conversion with unavoidable losses. The efficiency depends on actual electrical input and useful thermal output.

## 19. Steady-Flow Energy Equation (SFEE)
For steady flow, the energy balance includes heat transfer, work, enthalpy, kinetic energy and potential energy. A simplified heater form can be written as P_electrical≈m_dot c_p(Tout−Tin)+Qdot_loss when kinetic and potential energy changes are negligible. This form is useful when water is continuously entering and leaving the storage heater.

## 20. Application of SFEE to the Water-Heater System
During frequent use, normal water enters the heater and hot water leaves through the outlet. The field temperatures indicate Tin≈37 °C and Tout>45 °C. Therefore the temperature rise across the observed delivery condition is greater than 8 K. A numerical SFEE flow-rate calculation requires a measured mass flow rate, which has not been supplied and is intentionally left as a future measurement.

## 21. Flow of Water Through the System
The simplified flow path is: water supply → inlet → storage heater → heating element/storage volume → hot-water outlet → shower or tap. When hot water is withdrawn, fresh water enters to replace it. Because the 58 heaters are distributed across seven hostels, hostel energy is obtained by summing the energy of individual units rather than assuming one central heating plant.

## 22. Heat Transfer and Modes of Heat Loss
Conduction occurs through tank walls, metal components and pipes. Convection occurs between the external heater surfaces and air and within the water. Radiation occurs from warm external surfaces to the surrounding environment. Insulation reduces heat transfer from the storage tank. The three modes explain why electrical input is not converted entirely into useful water heating.

## 23. Thermodynamic Properties of Water
Key properties for the study are temperature, pressure, density, specific heat, internal energy and enthalpy. The working reference values are Tin≈37 °C, Tout>45 °C, representative capacity 25 L, mass≈25 kg and cp≈4.18 kJ/kg·K. The calculations use 45 °C only as a conservative minimum reference because the actual outlet temperature was observed to be higher.

## 24. Energy Losses and System Efficiency
The ideal useful heat is Q_useful=mc_pΔT. Actual efficiency is η=(Q_useful/E_electrical)×100. An actual efficiency percentage should not be reported until both useful thermal output and electrical input are measured consistently. Losses may arise through tank walls, pipes, standby heat transfer, incomplete conversion and other practical effects. The project therefore avoids the previous sample efficiency value and keeps efficiency as an experimental target.

## 25. Experimental Observation and Data Collection
The field survey covered seven girls' hostels and 58 heaters. The observed distribution was 8, 8, 8, 8, 16, 4 and 6 heaters for Hostels 1–7 respectively. Normal water was measured at approximately 37 °C and heated outlet water at more than 45 °C. Hot-water availability was observed at approximately 5 minutes after prolonged non-use and approximately 1 minute during frequent usage. These are field observations and should be retained as such.

## 26. Thermodynamic Calculations and Performance Analysis
The reference calculation for 25 kg of water heated from 37 °C to 45 °C gives Q=836 kJ=0.232 kWh. A 2 kW heater has a conditional connected-load contribution of 2 kW. If all 58 heaters were 2 kW units, the maximum connected load would be 116 kW; this is not actual simultaneous demand. Hostel-wise energy is the sum of the operating energy of its heaters. Further performance analysis requires exact brand counts, operating duration and electrical meter readings.

## 27. Energy-Saving Improvements and Engineering Recommendations
Recommended measures include improving tank insulation, insulating hot-water pipes, avoiding unnecessary heating, using appropriate thermostat settings, maintaining the heating element, reducing heat loss to the surroundings and monitoring energy consumption. Hostel 5, with 16 heaters, is a useful priority for energy monitoring because it has the largest number of units. Any intervention should preserve safe operation and follow manufacturer guidance.

## 28. Conclusion and Thermodynamic Significance
The seven-hostel survey demonstrates the practical application of thermodynamics to a distributed electric storage water-heater system. The system contains 58 heaters across seven hostels, with V-Guard and Venus units observed. Field measurements showed approximately 37 °C normal water and more than 45 °C heated outlet water, with hot-water availability observations of approximately 5 minutes after prolonged non-use and approximately 1 minute during frequent use. The reference calculation gives 836 kJ (0.232 kWh) for 25 kg of water raised from 37 °C to 45 °C. The study connects the Zeroth and First Laws, heat transfer, internal energy, enthalpy, specific heat, SFEE, flow and efficiency with an actual engineering system. Future measurements of exact temperature-time curves, flow rate and electrical energy consumption will allow experimental efficiency and energy-performance values to replace reference calculations.
